However, environmental impacts associated with  ENMs and their manufacture 2 , 3  could potentially offset the intended  benefits. While materials scientists are accustomed to optimizing  functional performance and price, minimizing human health or  environmental impacts a priori has not been systematically pur- sued 3 . Thus, ENMs and ENM-enabled products originally intended  to solve societal challenges could result in unintended consequences,  threatening the long-term commercial viability of ENMs due to real  or perceived risks. Furthermore, as the rate of material innovation  is rapid and relevant regulations for systematic assessment are lag- ging 4 , health and environmental impact assessments are challenged  to keep pace and are therefore conducted on a case-by-case basis. The significant economic and performance potential for ENM- enabled products is reflected by the United States (US) National  Nanotechnology Initiative (NNI) annual investment, totalling  approximately US$15 billion from 2007 to 2015 (Fig.  1 ). Over this  same time period, the annual NNI investment in environment,  health and safety (EHS) research ranged from US$48.3-120.2 mil- lion, never exceeding 8% of the total NNI investment (Fig.  1 ) 5 - 13 .  This disparity underscores the challenge facing designers who aim  to develop sustainable ENMs and ENM-enabled products that  maximize functional performance while minimizing adverse envi- ronmental or human health impacts. The data necessary to system- atically assess safety before commercialization are lacking, which  has historically led to two 'fatal' outcomes for emerging technolo- gies: (1) threatened acceptance due to public perception of a risk  in the absence of high-quality data, or (2) delayed observation of  adverse impacts, leading to 'regrettable substitutions' 14 . The most  notable example of the former is the effective elimination of the  genetically modified organisms market across much of Europe 15 ,  demonstrating the need for improved design guidelines that support  simultaneous consideration of functional performance, price and  environmental impact. Such systematic assessments of trade-offs  are imperative to inform sustainable design of emerging products,  while prospective evaluations are necessary to minimize unfore- seen implications associated with material selection and product  design decisions. There have been multiple strategies proposed to address these  trade-offs in nanotechnology and nano-enabled products, includ- ing multi-criteria decision analysis (MCDA) coupled with life-cycle  assessment (LCA) 16 , 17  and tools such as the LICARA (Life Cycle and  Risk Assessment) nanoSCAN 18 . While valuable, these tools can be  too qualitative and limited in nuance within each class of nanoma- terial to inform design decisions. Additionally, LCAs are time and  resource intensive, and result in potentially high levels of uncertainty  given limited data available for nanotechnology. Instead, sustainable  nanomaterial selection analogous to those used by mechanical and  materials engineers, such as the well-established Ashby materials  selection framework 19 , 20 , is developed and explored here. Simplified, Ashby advises a stepwise process to inform mate- rial choice by delineating (multi)parametric design requirements  and then screening vast material libraries to curate a selection of  viable candidates. Zimmerman    1,5 * Engineered nanomaterials (ENMs) and ENM-enabled products have emerged as potentially high-performance replacements to  conventional materials and chemicals. As such, there is an urgent need to incorporate environmental and human health objec- tives into ENM selection and design processes. Here, an adapted framework based on the Ashby material selection strategy is  presented as an enhanced selection and design process, which includes functional performance as well as environmental and  human health considerations. The utility of this framework is demonstrated through two case studies, the design and selection  of antimicrobial substances and conductive polymers, including ENMs, ENM-enabled products and their alternatives. Further,  these case studies consider both the comparative efficacy and impacts at two scales: (i) a broad scale, where chemical/mate- rial classes are readily compared for primary decision-making, and (ii) within a chemical/material class, where physicochemical  properties are manipulated to tailor the desired performance and environmental impact profile. Development and implemen- tation of this framework can inform decision-making for the implementation of ENMs to facilitate promising applications and  prevent unintended consequences. This is a well-established  approach used by both product designers and materials scientists,  and its expansion to include nanomaterials has also been previously  suggested 1 . However, the systematic evaluation of ENM product  performance, and the influence of embedding those ENMs into  other carriers (that is, metamaterials), has limited development of  ENM-specific selection diagrams. Further, while there have been  efforts to use Ashby's material selection framework to assess the  environmental implications of some bulk materials 21  and compos- ites 22 , understanding the functional performance and environmen- tal impacts of ENMs simultaneously has not been comprehensively  pursued. Recently, Ashby expanded metamaterial performance  metrics to incorporate environmental life cycle impacts, such as  cumulative energy demand (CED), water use and greenhouse gas  emissions 21 , but notably, there is an opportunity for the inclusion of  omitted metrics (for example, toxicity). As such, there is a need to  further enhance the framework to include inherent material hazard  and environmental or human health implications. The emerging  data associated with LCAs, toxicity and functional performance in  the novel domain of ENM-enabled products can be harnessed to  develop a material selection and design strategy to guide the design  of sustainable ENM and ENM-enabled products. Here, we demonstrate the potential of this strategy via two case  studies: (1) selecting between ENMs and bulk chemicals for anti- microbial applications and (2) selecting an ENM to nano-enable a  material for high electrical conductivity applications. In addition to  the functional performance, design metrics include human health  impacts using zebrafish toxicity as a model organism 23 , 24 , cradle-to- gate CED and material price. The approach presented here informs  where best to focus limited time and resources in determining the  viability of an ENM for a given application. For example, instead of  investing in more complicated toxicity assays across multiple tro- phic levels, or relying on the uncertain nature of life-cycle toxic- ity assessment, the candidate materials can be curated to those that  meet goals set for functionality and sustainability. The success of  this approach will rely on a collaboration between materials scien- tists creating the nanomaterials, environmental scientists evaluating  the nanomaterials, and product designers to ensure that (1) robust  data are generated relating functional benefits to ENMs and their  properties and (2) environmental implications of emerging ENMs  can be well understood and communicated, so future iterations of  nanomaterials and nano-enabled products can be designed to avoid  these issues. In the absence of this concerted effort, nanotechnology  may not realize its full potential to have a lasting, positive impact on  science and society. Adapted framework for sustainable ENMs The potential to support sustainable ENM selection and design  through the proposed framework is demonstrated through two case  studies. The first highlights the use of ENMs versus bulk chemical  substances for antimicrobial activity and the second is applied to  ENM selection for nano-enabled polyaniline (PANI) for improved  electrical conductivity. These case studies were selected due to real  innovation needs driven by regulatory changes, as in the case of  recently banned chemical antimicrobials 25 , or market demands, as  in the case of ENM-PANI composites for flexible supercapacitors 26 .  The specific ENMs, chemicals and materials included in each case  study were chosen based on their current use and/or promise in real- izing the desired functional performance goals, as well as the quan- tity and quality of data available on human health impacts, life-cycle  environmental impacts and material price (Supplementary Table 1). ENM versus bulk chemical antimicrobial alternatives.  To serve  as a viable alternative to bulk chemicals or materials, ENMs should  realize the same or enhanced functional performance while mini- mizing potential adverse impacts. Select chemical antimicrobials,  including triclosan and triclocarban, were recently banned by the  US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) 25 . This has increased  interest in ENM-based antimicrobials, which have shown efficacy  in water treatment and medical devices 27 , but still require more  robust environmental and human health impact assessments. As a first step, designers must meet a specific functional perfor- mance goal when choosing substance alternatives. For example, for  antimicrobial agents, the performance metric could be antimicrobial  activity against a specific organism (for example,  Escherichia coli )  in terms of minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC, mg l -1 ).  Three implications are also evaluated to understand the trade-offs  between the beneficial aspects of each material and its detrimen- tal effects. The first is the price (in US$ kg -1 ) that product design- ers pay to nanomaterial producers for the ENMs in their product.  Nanomaterial price is a useful screening tool for nanomaterials  selection because it is ubiquitously available and intercomparable.  However, we note critically that price does not reflect the total cost  to the user (that is, materials with lower starting material prices may  translate to higher total cost to the manufacturer, due to various dif- ferences in the processing or other considerations, and this should  be kept in mind when applying the selection strategy). The second  implication metric is the CED (in MJ kg -1 ) that comes from the pro- duction of nanomaterials. This is calculated from cradle-to-gate via  life-cycle assessment, where the most sustainable material is the one  that requires the least amount of energy during production. This  includes electrical and heat energy during production, as well as the  embodied energy of the precursors and reagents. The third consid- eration is hazard to human health. Life-cycle toxicity assessment  does not account for physicochemical differences in nanomateri- als, and does not always have the background data from experi- mental toxicity assays to support its assumptions 28 , while toxicity  assessment across multiple trophic levels of organisms is expensive  and time-intensive. Therefore, in this study, relative human health  hazard is indicated by the results of embryonic zebrafish toxicity  assays (LC 50 , the concentration of a material that is lethal to 50% of  a population, mg l -1 ), where the least toxic material is the one with  the highest LC 50  value. Since risk is a function of hazard and expo- sure 29 , product designers can minimize risk by minimizing inherent  hazard regardless of the circumstances of the exposure scenarios.  Further, hazard is directly related to the physicochemical proper- ties of a material, which can be tuned (see section 'Approach' in the  Supplementary Information for more information), while exposure  requires numerous assumptions, the breadth of which would be dif- ficult to capture in an Ashby chart. When considering antimicrobial efficacy and price, zinc oxide  nanoparticles (nZnO) are economically competitive over some  bulk chemical alternatives due to the combination of a relatively  low MIC and unit price 30  ( http://www.ebiochem.com/ ,  https:// www.tcichemicals.com/en/us/ ) (Fig.  3b ). When significant ranges exist within a material class, it sug- gests the opportunity to tune physiochemical properties to  impart the desired outcome. As one example, multiwalled carbon  nanotubes (MWCNTs) can either be one of the most promising  or least effective antimicrobial agents, depending on the surface  chemistry of the material 31 , 32  (Fig.  2a ). Similarly, variability in  ENM morphology (for example, size and shape) imparts a wide  range in antimicrobial activity and embryonic zebrafish toxic- ity values, especially for nZnO and nAg, where the mechanism  of action is at least partially related to ion production 33 , 34 , whose  dissolution rate is fundamentally related to surface area, strain  or curvature, and crystallinity 35 . The wide ranges in CED values,  especially for ENMs, are related to the large variety of synthe- sis routes for each material. These different synthesis routes are  used to make ENMs of variable size or morphology, as is the case  for many non-carbonaceous ENMs, or to synthesize materials of  variable purity or structural quality, as is the case for carbona- ceous ENMs 36 . The impact of these physicochemical differences  and how they can be understood to inform material design is dis- cussed further below. When selecting a substance, one is often evaluating trade-offs  or attempting a co- or multi-optimization scheme, and two-dimen- sional (2D) bubble charts (for example, relating the functional  performance parameter to an environmental implication param- eter) enable this multi-criteria comparison (Fig.  3 ). For example,  a designer with established constraints of high efficacy and low  hazard can quickly eliminate several options (that is, substances  outside the upper left region in Fig.  3a ), including triclosan and tri- clocarban, while still providing a variety of viable alternatives (for  example, MWCNTs, nZnO, nAg and 4-chlorophenol). Given a basic  constraint for performance or benefit, further specifications can be  set for the maximum tolerable impact (for example, CED or mate- rial price; Fig.  3b,c ) based on regulatory limitations, public pressure,  industrial norms and the influence of other stakeholders. Similarly,  multi-criterion thresholds can be set (for example, simultaneous  optimization of performance, impact and price), and can be visu- alized in a multi-dimensional material parameter space (Fig.  3d ).  This concept can be naturally extended to  n  dimensions aided by a  digital platform 37 . In this example, nZnO emerges as a viable substi- tute due to the low price, high LC 50  (low hazard) and relatively low  MIC (high efficacy). Comparing ENM-enabled composite materials for conductive  polymers.  Polymers are enabled with ENMs for many reasons,  including to reduce ENM release, to provide stability from envi- ronmental transformation, or to enhance a functional performance  property, such as the electrical conductivity of intrinsically conduct- ing polymers such as PANI 38 . The ENM type and mass loading criti- cally influence the net impact on conductivity, price and CED of the  composite material (Fig.  4a,b  and see Supplementary Fig. 1 for 1D  visualization). Note that inherent material hazard is not considered  here due to challenges in data availability, particularly as it relates to  ENM release and exposure, and the lack of understanding regard- ing the toxicity of the composites themselves 39 . From the selection  diagrams (Fig.  4 ), a constraint requiring a 10-fold higher conductiv- ity for a PANI-ENM composite compared with neat PANI (Fig.  4b ,  constraint 1), and a stipulation that the CED of the additional ENM  does not exceed 100 MJ kg -1  (Fig.  4b , constraint 2) can be set. These  constraints narrow the available materials to single-walled carbon  nanotubes (SWCNTs), MWCNTs and nTiO 2 . Seeking to minimize  the ENM price further narrows the choice to MWCNTs and nTiO 2 ,  focusing innovation efforts to just two viable material candidates  (see 3D co-optimization, Fig.  4c ). In this way, performance, nega- tive impact and material price can be considered simultaneously to  facilitate sustainable product design. Future considerations While this strategy, like Ashby's, offers a promising way to select  sustainable (nano)materials, all such approaches present some  limitations 42 . Material selection charts, including this framework,  are not meant to be deterministic tools to identify the optimal mate- rial for a specific application, but are instead meant to focus limited  time and resources on a more promising and curated pool of mate- rial candidates. This pool itself may be constrained by the nature of  the material considered; that is, material classes such as composites  and alloys will not be comprehensively captured given the infinite  possible material combinations. ENMs present further challenges  to this approach in that small changes in any number of physio- chemical structures or properties can significantly influence func- tional performance as well as hazard implications. This can result in  a relatively large 'bubble' for a given nanomaterial class, thus con- founding material selection due to overlap. As such, it is important  that these approaches are part of a multiple step process in selecting  appropriate (nano)materials by eliminating those that are not viable  from consideration early on in the design process. The expansion of material selection charts is essential for rational  sustainable design of ENMs and ENM-enabled products. Likewise, designers cannot consider environmental impacts during  development and commercialization because the appropriate data  for their system may not be available. By defining a common set of  data needs, limited nanoEHS research resources can focus on crucial  data gaps to inform the development of design guidelines for sustain- able ENMs and ENM-enabled products. In turn, materials scientists  and engineers will have guidance toward the level of improvement  they must achieve (via enhanced performance or mitigated implica- tions) to be functionally competitive with the existing substances. To realize these benefits, the relevant stakeholders must (1) agree  on the type of data needed to efficiently construct material libraries  and (2) systematize the data quality standards 43 , so that all materials  can be evaluated through the same or directly comparable process.  Such standard practices exist for most traditional materials charac- terization approaches (for example, the ASTM methods for tensile  strength 44 ), but are lacking with respect to many of the novel appli- cations of ENMs and for environmental impacts generally 45 - 47 . That  is, disparate metrics or approaches reduce the synergistic power  of the collective community to uncover parametric or fundamen- tal relationships between ENM structure, properties and hazards 3 .  The collection and inclusion of more data for consistent metrics  using consensus protocols will improve chart utility for the general  selection of chemical and material classes and will inform design.  Similarly, criterion for environmental metrics beyond embodied  energy, material recyclability and carbon footprint 21 , such as eco- toxicity and cumulative water demand, are still under development.  Further, improved consistency in ENM characterization and metrics  can lead to the emergence of software-based platforms coupled to  materials prediction from theory, which may ultimately include or  enable calculation of hybridized and decorated materials. However,  visualization of all of the possible materials space simultaneously  may obfuscate the value of the Ashby strategy. Indeed, future users  will likely have to specify what part of the materials space they wish  to access and constrain whether or not they are including param- eters calculated from theory or measured via experiment. Conclusions Leveraging conventional material selection frameworks that are  focused on performance and material price, and which are already  familiar to materials scientists and engineers, to consider ENMs and  their environmental and human health impacts will be increasingly  important as the ENM enterprise continues to develop and mature.  The systematic selection strategy presented here offers multi-fac- eted practical benefits including (1) selecting the optimum sub- stance considering a variety of attributes (for example, performance,  environmental and human health implications, price of materials)  from the currently available ENM, chemical and material libraries,  (2) addressing real or perceived risks, and (3) avoiding regrettable  substitution. This framework brings value to the material selection  process, and supports curation of the ever-expanding nanomaterial  library focusing limited resources on the most promising ENMs.  Further, there are broadly transformative impacts of the proposed  framework, such as highlighting data gaps to motivate critically  needed research and developing tools to inform the design of ENMs  and ENM-enabled products. This will contribute to the nanotech- nology enterprise realizing its full potential to address societal chal- lenges while doing so in a way that is both resilient, in the short  term, and sustainable for future generations.
