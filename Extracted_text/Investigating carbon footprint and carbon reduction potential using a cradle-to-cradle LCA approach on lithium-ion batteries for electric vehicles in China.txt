Investigating carbon footprint and carbon reduction potential using a  cradle-to-cradle LCA approach on lithium-ion batteries for electric vehicles  in China  Quanwei Chen   a , Xin Lai   a , * , Huanghui Gu   a , Xiaopeng Tang   b , Furong Gao   b , Xuebing Han   c ,  Yuejiu Zheng   a  a  School of Mechanical Engineering, University of Shanghai for Science and Technology, Shanghai, 200093, China  b  Department of Chemical and Biological Engineering, The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, Hong Kong, China  c  State Key Laboratory of Automotive Safety and Energy, Tsinghua University, Beijing, 100084, China    A R T I C L E  I N F O    Handling Editor: Zhen Leng   Keywords:  Life cycle assessment  Carbon footprint  Carbon emission reduction  Lithium-ion batteries  Electric vehicles  A B S T R A C T    Lithium-ion battery (LIB) is one of the core components of electric vehicles (EVs), and its ecological impacts are  significant for the sustainable development of EVs. In this study, the carbon footprint of LIBs produced in China is  investigated using a cradle-to-cradle life-cycle assessment approach. The results can be summarized as follows:  (1) The carbon emission from battery production is 91.21 kg CO 2 -eq/kWh, in which the cathode production and  battery assembly process are the main sources of carbon emissions; (2) The carbon emission during the battery  use phase under China ' s electricity mix which is dominated by thermal power in 2020 is 154.1 kg CO 2 -eq/kWh;  (3) Hydrometallurgy is the battery recycling method with the lowest carbon emission, and direct physical  recycling method has attractive carbon reduction potential. (4) The carbon emission of battery remanufacturing  through recycled materials is 51.8% lower than that of battery production with raw materials. Under the future  electricity mix, the carbon emissions of battery production in 2050 and 2060 will be reduced by 75% and 84.9%,  respectively. The short-term carbon reduction measure is battery recycling, and the long-term carbon reduction  measure is the greening of electricity.    1. Introduction  Global climate change is posing a significant threat to human society.  As a major greenhouse gas (GHG) associated with global warming,  carbon dioxide will bring a series of problems, such as ice-sheet melt,  sea-level rise, and ecological environment damage ( Golledge et al.,  2019 ). The Paris Agreement has set a temperature control target of  1.5  ◦ C, and more than 190 countries have submitted  “ intended nation­ ally determined contributions ” , indicating that they will achieve the  economy-wide goal of reducing GHG emissions ( Tanaka et al., 2021 ).  Transportation is the second-largest GHG emission source in the world  ( Elgowainy et al., 2018 ), which has attracted widespread attention in  carbon emission reduction. Vigorously promoting electric vehicles is an  effective path to reducing GHG emissions and energy consumption,  which has been adopted by governments around the world ( Knobloch  et al., 2020 ;  Lai et al., 2021b ). The rapid development of EVs has  brought about large-scale applications of power batteries ( Lai et al.,  2021e ). Lithium-ion batteries are the first choice for EVs because of their  high energy density, high working voltage, low self-discharge rate, long  life cycle, and almost zero memory effect ( Lai, X. et al., 2022 ;  Lai et al.,  2021d ). It is estimated that the global demand for LIBs will reach 9300  GWh by 2030 ( Yuan et al., 2021 ), and the installed capacity of power  LIBs has exceeded that of the electronic consumer market driven by the  rapid development of EVs ( Li et al., 2021 ). China ' s power battery pro­ duction and sales ranked first in the world in 2014, which reached 220  GWh in 2021 with a year-on-year increase of 165%.  Fig. 1  shows that the  annual installed capacity of power batteries in China will reach about  1.5 TWh by 2030 ( Union ). However, the rapid growth of power battery  production and use brings the problem of resource shortage and energy  consumption, resulting in direct or indirect GHG emissions. Compared  with the developed countries, the pressure on low-carbon energy in  China is more significant. China is the world ' s largest producer and user  of LIBs. Therefore, it is urgent to accurately calculate the carbon emis­ sion of LIBs in the whole life cycle in China under various electricity  * Corresponding author. Life cycle assessment (LCA) is a method to assess the environmental  burden in the life cycle of products, processes, or activities ( Silvestri  et al., 2020 ). Currently, LCA is widely used to evaluate environmental  pollution and GHG emissions of various battery technologies, and they  can be summarized as follows: (1) Comparing carbon emissions and  environmental indicators between LIBs and other batteries. The results  show that solid-state batteries produce lower carbon emissions than LIBs  ( Smith et al., 2020 ). Compared with the Ni - H battery and solar cell, the  carbon emission of the raw material assembly process of LIBs is the  lowest, and it has better environmental friendliness ( Liang et al., 2017 ).  Moreover, almost all environmental impact indicators of LIBs are lower  than those of lead-acid and Ni - Cd batteries ( Hammond and Hazeldine,  2015 ;  Yang et al., 2020 ). (2) Comparison of carbon emissions and  environmental indicators of LIBs with different material systems. The  previous study ( Vandepaer et al., 2017 ) showed that the GHG emissions  and ozone consumption of lithium metal polymer batteries in the pro­ duction stage are greater than those of the traditional LIBs. The carbon  emission of Lithium-iron phosphate (LFP) batteries is about 42.0 - 44.5%  lower than that of LIBs with nickel-cobalt-manganese oxide (NCM), and  the value of water footprint and ecological footprint of LFP is lower ( Wu  et al., 2019 ). Compared with the NCM battery, the LFP battery shown  less environmental burden in the production phase but a more signifi­ cant environmental burden in the use and transportation phases ( Shu  et al., 2021 ). (3) Carbon emissions and environmental impacts of LIBs at  different phases. The early LCA studies mainly focused on evaluating  battery production and use stages due to the lack of recycling process  data ( Liang et al., 2017 ;  Vandepaer et al., 2017 ;  Wang, C. et al., 2017 ).  The studies found that the carbon emission and environmental impact of  the battery production stage dominate the whole life cycle of LIBs  ( Porzio and Scown, 2021 ;  Vandepaer et al., 2017 ;  Wang, C. et al., 2017 ).  The environmental impact of the battery in the usage stage is mainly  related to the electricity mix and the performance of the battery, such as  the aging rate, charge, and discharge efficiency ( Lybbert et al., 2021 ;  Vandepaer et al., 2017 ).  With the rapid growth of EVs and the attention to global warming,  the carbon emissions of LIBs in the whole life cycle are becoming the  focus of governments, enterprises, and research institutions worldwide.  However, the existing study results are quite different, and the carbon  emission in the production phase ranges from 36.9 to 313 kg CO 2 -eq/  kWh, with an average of 110 kg CO 2 -eq/kWh ( Peters et al., 2017 ;  Temporelli et al., 2020 ). The main reasons for the differences are that  each study may be based on different battery life cycle inventory data,  and some studies did not trace the upstream process data of the battery  materials. In addition, few studies focus on the LCA study on battery  remanufacturing, which refers to reusing recycled materials from waste  LIBs in battery manufacturing. Note that the carbon footprint and car­ bon emissions are two indicators that are often mentioned ( Lai et al.,  2022 ), and the former has a larger scope than the latter. Generally, the  carbon footprint is defined as the total amount of GHG emissions that  come from the production, use, and end-of-life of a product or service,  and the carbon emission is the GHG emission of a product or service at a  particular stage or time. Because LIBs have a long-life cycle with  different stages, it is necessary to investigate the carbon footprint of LIBs  in this study and the potential carbon reduction measures proposed.  The current battery LCA studies mainly focus on the raw material  extraction to the disposal (cradle-to-grave) or the battery production  stage (cradle-to-gate) ( Kim et al., 2016 ;  Peters et al., 2017 ;  Sanf ´ elix  et al., 2015 ), which are the typical open-loop LCA methods. With the  development of battery material recycling and remanufacturing, the  cradle-to-cradle method is attracting more and more attention. Specif­ ically, the cradle-to-cradle method is a new full-lifecycle LCA method  considering reusing waste battery materials and battery remanufacture.  In this study, a cradle-to-cradle LCA method is developed to investigate  the carbon footprint of LIBs in China under the reality and future sce­ narios, and some valuable carbon reduction measures are given. The  study object is the LiNi 0.8 Co 0.1 Mn 0.1 O 2  (NCM811) battery, which is  widely used in EVs. The potential contributions of this paper are sum­ marized as follows:   (1) The cradle-to-cradle LCA is conducted on the NCM811 battery  produced and used in China.   (2) The carbon footprint in the full lifespan of LIBs, including battery  production considering raw materials, use, recycling, and rema­ nufacturing stages, are investigated in detail, and some valuable  carbon reduction measures are suggested.   (3) The carbon emissions of three typical battery recycling methods  are calculated and compared, followed by the carbon emission of  the battery remanufacturing based on recycled materials and that  of the battery production based on raw materials are compared.  The results can quantitatively describe the carbon emission  benefits of various battery recycling methods.   (4) The carbon emissions of battery production under the future  electricity mixes are calculated, and the goal of carbon neutrali­ zation during battery production is prospected.  The study results are beneficial to determining the key factors  affecting carbon footprint in the full lifespan of LIBs, and form some  effective carbon emission reduction strategies facing China ' s carbon  neutralization strategy. The remainder of this paper is organized as  follows. Section  2  introduces the LCA approach. In Section  3 , the carbon  footprint of LIBs from cradle to cradle is calculated and discussed in rich  scenarios, followed by some value carbon reduction measures are sug­ gested. Finally, some conclusions are provided in Section  4 .  2. Goal and scope  The study aims to quantify the carbon footprint of LIBs in the whole  life cycle, assess the potential of carbon emission reduction in different  stages, and investigate the carbon emissions in the production and use  stages of LIBs for the carbon peak and carbon neutralization scenarios.  The study results provide a valuable reference for the whole LIB industry  chain to achieve the peak of carbon emission in 2030 and carbon  neutralization in 2060. In this study, the carbon footprint model of the  NCM811 battery from cradle to cradle is constructed based on the Gabi  software version 10.6, and the carbon emission and its distribution at  each stage are calculated and discussed.  2.1.1. Functional unit  The functional unit is used to formally define the studied product  according to its function and allows unified analysis of several stages  according to a functionally equivalent basis ( Vandepaer et al., 2017 ). In  this study, an NCM 811 battery is taken as the study object because of its  wide application in EVs. The functional unit is defined as 1 kWh of the  NCM811 battery for an EV, which fully ensures the quantitative evalu­ ation and comparability of carbon emissions at each stage in the full life  cycle of LIBs for EVs. In this study, a battery pack with the capacity of 74  kWh in an EV with a service life of 8 years and an overall driving range of  120,000 km is taken as the research object during the battery use phase.  2.1.2. System boundary  From the perspective of the whole life cycle of the battery, the scope  of the LCA for LIBs should be from the raw material extraction (cradle)  to the disposal (grave), it is sometimes referred to as  “ cradle-to-grave ” .  However, most existing studies about the LCA of LIBs focus on the  battery production stage, which is often called the  “ cradle-to-gate ”  ( Kim  et al., 2016 ). In recent years, with the continuous increase of retired LIBs  ( Lai et al., 2021a ), the study on the environmental impacts of different  battery recycling methods has increased ( Lin et al., 2021 ;  Rinne et al.,  2021 ). The above research on the whole life cycle of LIBs is often  referred to as  “ cradle to grave ” . To the best of our knowledge, few  studies have focused on the carbon footprint of batteries from cradle to  cradle (i.e., considering the recycling and remanufacturing of battery  materials). The system boundary of the study is shown in  Fig. 2 . A  complete closed-loop  “ cradle-to-cradle ”  approach for the carbon foot­ print calculation of the NCM811 battery system is performed following  the standard LCA procedures. The whole system mainly includes the  extraction and processing of raw materials, the component manufacture  of battery, battery production, the use phase of battery, and the recy­ cling and disposal of battery. Noted that the carbon footprint of battery  transportation and storage is beyond the scope of this study due to their  complexity.  2.2. Life cycle inventory analysis  Life cycle inventory (LCI) analysis is an essential step in creating  input and output flow lists for a product system. The flows include inputs  of water, energy, and raw materials, the outputs of products, and  emissions to air, land, and water. Technically, LCI is the data collection  portion of LCA, which can be obtained through experiments, enterprise  research, literatures, and other ways, and the background data of up­ stream materials can be supplemented and corrected through LCI  databases.  2.2.1. Inventory of battery production phase  The capacity of the investigated battery pack containing 188 cells is  74 kWh, and the weight and capacity of each cell are 1.74 kg and 116  Ah, respectively. The detailed information is listed in  Table S1 .  Fig. 3  lists the material composition of a cell, including cathode, anode,  separator, electrolyte, current collector, casing, and other materials.  These data mainly come from our laboratory disassemble tests, and the  LCI data of upstream material production is listed in  Tables S3 - S14 . The  test results reveal that the 1 kWh battery contains 559 g of cathode  material, accounting for 34.43% of the total material inputs. The cath­ ode is produced by the reaction of ternary precursor (Ni 0.8 C­ o 0.1 Mn 0.1 (OH) 2 ) and lithium hydroxide using the high-temperature  solid-phase method, co-precipitation method, and hydrothermal  method ( Gu et al., 2021 ). The ternary precursor is obtained from the  mixed solution of manganese sulfate, nickel sulfate, and cobalt sulfate by  adding liquid alkali (NaOH) and ammonia ( Lai et al., 2022 ). Lithium  hydroxide is the basic material for preparing ternary LIBs, which are  mainly derived from lithium ore and lithium-containing brine ( Wang, H.  et al., 2017 ). The demand for anode material in batteries is second only  to that of cathode material, which is 385 g/kWh, accounting for 22.13%  of the total materials. The anode material is artificial graphite, which is  obtained by high-temperature graphitization of natural graphite and  pitch.  The battery separator in this study is 30 g/kWh, accounting for  2.87% of the total materials, and its raw materials mainly include  polyolefins, polymer materials, and inorganic materials. The electrolyte  (324 g/kWh) accounts for 18.62% of the input materials of the NCM811  battery, and it is prepared from lithium hexafluorophosphate (LiPF 6 ),  ethylene carbonate (EC), and ethyl methyl carbonate (EMC) through  low-temperature crystallization, filtration, and drying. The current  collector of the NCM811 battery mainly includes two parts: the current  collector aluminum foil of the cathode and the current collector copper  foil of the anode. The cathode collector of the NCM811 battery uses  aluminum (58 g/kWh), accounting for 3.33% and the anode collector  uses copper (129 g/kWh), accounting for 7.41% of the input materials.  Fig. 2. Inventory of battery recycling phase  At present, there are four main battery recycling methods, namely  pyrometallurgy, hydrometallurgy, pyro- and hydrometallurgy, and  direct physical recycling ( Fan et al., 2020 ;  Qian et al., 2022 ).  Fig. 5  shows the pyrometallurgical technology route studied in this study. In  pyrometallurgy, waste LIBs are melted in a high-temperature furnace.  The valuable metals (nickel-cobalt-manganese) react with the reducing  agents/additives to be reduced to cobalt-nickel-copper alloy products  for recycling ( Rajaeifar et al., 2021 ). In the process, lithium and  aluminum are turned into slag and discarded, and the separator, elec­ trolyte, and electrolyte are burned. It is a battery recycling technology  route that has been widely commercialized.  The technical route of the combined pyro- and hydrometallurgy  recycling batteries is shown in  Fig. 5  ( Doose et al., 2021 ). After the  batteries are discharged, disassembled, and crushed, they are  heat-treated at different temperatures to recover the high-value cobalt  and nickel materials, and lithium is recovered through the further hy­ drometallurgical process. Such as the LCA of Umicore ' s VAL ' EAS ™  process is a typical pyro- and hydrometallurgy method ( Fan et al.,  2020 ). The hydrometallurgical process has the characteristics of high  recycling efficiency, low energy consumption, simple reagent operation,  and relatively mature technology. It ' s the mainstream process used by  Chinese Enterprises. After the batteries are discharged, disassembled,  and pulverized, the final product is obtained by hydrometallurgy  through low-temperature leaching, purification, and separation. The  leaching process includes alkali leaching and acid leaching, and the  technical route of acid leaching is more mature and widely used in  commercialization. However, strong acids and other chemical reagents  in the acid leaching process will generate considerable secondary  pollution, such as toxic gas and waste acid solution. Carbon emission results  The carbon emissions of the battery production phase are calculated  using Gabi 10.6 software, and the results are shown in  Fig. 6 . It can be  observed that the total carbon emission during the battery production  phase is 91.21 kg CO 2 -eq/kWh, and the carbon emission from the pro­ duction of cathode materials (47.15 kg CO 2 -eq/kWh) accounts for the  most significant proportion (51.69%) during the battery production. The  primary contribution of carbon emissions of the cathode materials  comes from upstream processes related to nickel, cobalt, lithium  extraction, and beneficiation. As the lithium extraction technology has  not yet reached the level of industrial production and geographical  conditions, more than 70% of lithium relies on imports in China. Chile  has the largest lithium brine resource globally, followed by Bolivia and  Argentina, which account for nearly 80% of the world ' s brine reserves  ( Hu et al., 2021 ). The countries with rich brine have backward pro­ duction equipment and sizeable primary energy consumption, which is  the main carbon source. For countries rich in lithium mineral  concentrates, such as Australia, extracting lithium from minerals pro­ duces higher carbon emissions than extracting lithium from brine ( Kelly  et al., 2021 ). Cobalt is the most valuable component of batteries, and  two-thirds of the global cobalt supply is mined in the Democratic Re­ public of the Congo ( Baars et al., 2020 ). Due to the instability of the  national political situation and the backwardness of technology, many  carbon emissions will also be generated in cobalt mining. Compared  with cobalt, the global nickel reserves are widely distributed, in which  laterite nickel accounts for 60% of the global terrestrial nickel resources  and nickel sulfide ore accounts for 40%. The global nickel supply mainly  comes from a few countries with advantages in nickel and coal re­ sources, such as Indonesia. Nickel production in the above countries is  characterized by high temperature, low yield, and high fossil energy  consumption, which is also the main source of carbon emissions of  cathode materials.  The carbon emission of battery assembly (28.11 kg CO 2 -eq/kWh) is  the second-largest contributor to battery production, mainly from  electricity and natural gas. The carbon emission from electricity is 22.16  kg CO 2 -eq/kWh, which is related to the power structure. Here, the  average electricity mix of China in 2020 is considered: 67.9% of thermal  power, 17.0% of hydropower, 6.0% of wind power, 3.5% of photovoltaic  power generation, and 5.6% of other power generations ( CEC, 2021 ).  The other power generations include nuclear energy, geothermal en­ ergy, tidal energy, biomass energy, etc. As shown in  Fig. 6 , the carbon  emission of other parts is 15.95 kg CO 2 -eq/kWh, mainly including car­ bon emissions of the anode, separator, electrolyte, copper foil,  aluminum foil, casing, and auxiliary materials. In this study, the casing  accounts for the highest proportion in other parts, mainly because the  casing used in the battery is aluminum. Upstream raw materials and  energy of aluminum production, including electricity, natural gas, and  limestone, will produce indirect carbon emissions, while the energy  consumed in the process and chemical reactions will generate direct  carbon emissions. The production of aluminum in China is dominated by  electrolytic aluminum with high energy consumption and high emission,  and the power sector is the primary source of carbon emission in the  electrolytic aluminum industry chain, mainly due to the high proportion  of thermal power in China ' s energy structure. The results in  Fig. 6  show  that the carbon emission from the anode accounts for 23.13% of that  from other parts. The production of artificial graphite requires a high  temperature, a long duration of carbonization, and a graphitization  process, which will generate much carbon emissions. Comparison with the existing results  To verify the accuracy of the calculation results, the carbon emis­ sions of battery production in this study are compared with those in  existing studies, and the results are shown in  Fig. 7 . It can be observed  that the carbon emissions of the production of different batteries have  significant differences, ranging from 42 to 140 kg CO 2 -eq/kWh, and the  cathode, assembly, and other parts are different. In this study, the car­ bon emission of the NCM811 battery during production is 91.21 kg CO 2 -  eq/kWh. This is because the nickel content of the NCM811 battery is  high, and the energy consumption of nickel extraction and processing is  higher than that of cobalt and manganese ( Crenna et al., 2021 ). The  difference in carbon emissions during battery assembly is mainly due to  the difference in the power grid structure. The carbon emissions in the  assembly phase in this study are similar to those calculated by Dai et al.  ( Kelly et al., 2019 ), which are calculated based on China ' s electricity  mix. However, our calculations are very different from those calculated  by Ciez et al. ( Ciez and Whitacre, 2019 ), which are calculated based on  the electricity mix of the United States. The difference in carbon emis­ sion from other parts of the battery is mainly due to the different  background data sources ( Kelly et al., 2019 ;  Sun et al., 2020 ), and the  battery production in the early stage of commercialization used the  relatively backward production equipment ( Kim et al., 2016 ). As shown  in  Fig. 7 , our calculation results are within a reasonable range, showing  high reliability.  3.1.3. Combined with the new negative carbon technol­ ogy, the goal of carbon neutrality in battery production can be achieved  in 2060.  The improvement of production efficiency depends on various fac­ tors, such as production equipment, process, and economic benefits,  which is a potential measure to reduce carbon emissions. According to  our data survey in a leading battery manufacturer, the production effi­ ciency of the battery batch production lines is about 30 ppm (pieces per  minute) in China. In this study, the relationship between production  efficiency and carbon emissions in the battery assembly is investigated.  Considering the limitations of equipment and cost, it assumes that the  battery production efficiency is increased by 5 ppm every year, and the  maximum production efficiency is 50 ppm. As shown in  Fig. 8 (b), the  carbon emission at the battery assembly stage is 28.11 kg CO 2 -eq/kWh  at the current battery production efficiency of 30 ppm. When the pro­ duction efficiency is increased to 40 ppm, the carbon emission in the  battery assembly stage is 21.08 kg CO 2 -eq/kWh, which is 25% lower  than that at 30 ppm. When the production efficiency is 50 ppm, the  carbon emission in the battery assembly stage is 16.87 kg CO 2 -eq/kWh,  which is 40.0% lower than that at 30 ppm. With improved production  efficiency, the energy consumption in the battery assembly stage is  reduced, and the carbon emission is also reduced. It can be concluded  that the carbon emission in the battery production phase can be reduced  with the improvement of production efficiency to a certain extent.  However, due to the limitation of production technology, production  efficiency cannot be improved indefinitely, and the potential for carbon  emission reduction will enter the plateau period.  3.2. Carbon emission and carbon reduction potential during battery  recycling  3.3.1. Carbon emission results  According to the survey data in Section  2 , the LCA of battery material  recycling and remanufacturing is conducted in this study. Specifically,  the carbon emissions of pyrometallurgy, hydrometallurgy, and direct  physical recycling methods are calculated, and the results are shown in  Fig. 10 . It can be seen that the carbon emission of the pyrometallurgical  recycling process is 5.11 kg CO 2 -eq/kWh, which is the highest carbon  emission among the three recycling methods. In the process of pyro­ metallurgical, high-temperature smelting batteries with a temperature  of more than 1000  ◦ C are usually used. The carbon emission comes from  the fossil energy consumed in the metallurgical process, which can  produce many direct and indirect carbon emissions. In addition, the  graphite in the anode cannot be recycled by pyrometallurgy, and the  pyrolysis of graphite in a high-temperature environment will produce  carbon emissions. Although pyrometallurgy can bring economic value,  its ecological value is not ideal. The carbon emission of hydrometallurgy  is 2.68 kg CO 2 -eq/kWh, which is 47.6% lower than that of pyrometal­ lurgy. Compared with pyrometallurgy, hydrometallurgy carries out  multi-step chemical treatment under low-temperature conditions,  without high energy consumption and high carbon emission processes  such as high-temperature treatment, and the graphite in anode will not  be converted into carbon dioxide to increase carbon emission. Although  hydrometallurgy can realize closed-loop recycling, it usually includes  more than 10 major steps, which will generate a considerable number of  toxic gases and waste solutions ( Chen, J. et al., 2019 ). The carbon  emission of the direct physical recycling method is 3.65 kg CO 2 -eq/kWh,  which is 28.6% lower than that of the pyrometallurgical recycling  method, and 26.6% higher than that of the hydrometallurgical recycling  method. Since the products of direct physical recycling are materials  that can be directly used in battery production, the complicated material  reproduction steps and secondary pollution are reduced. The nickel-rich  material used in the repair process, such as Ni 0.83 Mn 0.09 Co 0.08 (OH) 2  or  Ni(OH) 2 , LiOH - Li 2 SO 4  in the heat treatment process, and energy con­ sumption are the main sources of carbon emission.  3.3.2. Carbon reduction potential of battery recycling and remanufacturing  In this study, the carbon emission reduction potential in the recycling  stage is evaluated by comparing the carbon emission of batteries made  of recycled materials with that of batteries made of raw materials.  Although the recycling process of battery materials will produce carbon  emissions, the recycled materials can be directly used to manufacture  batteries, avoiding the carbon emissions caused by the mining and  refining of raw materials.  Fig. 10  shows that the total carbon emission of  remanufacturing batteries with recycled materials by pyrometallurgy is  86.86 kg CO 2 -eq/kWh, which is 4.8% lower than that of batteries pro­ duced with raw materials. It can be seen that pyrometallurgy has a  relatively limited carbon reduction potential for LIB remanufacturing.  The carbon emission from the remanufacturing of LIBs using the recy­ cled materials from hydrometallurgical is 60.77 kg CO 2 -eq/kWh, which  is 33.47% lower than that of batteries produced using raw materials. It is  because the carbon emission of hydrometallurgy is much lower than that  of pyrometallurgy. Moreover, the recycling rate of hydrometallurgy is  higher than that of pyrometallurgy ( Zhuang et al., 2019 ). The carbon  emission of remanufactured batteries using direct physical recycling is  43.92 kg CO 2 -eq/kWh, which is 49.4% lower than that of pyrometal­ lurgy and 27.7% lower than that of hydrometallurgy. It shows the  highest carbon emission reduction potential of the three recycling  methods. More importantly, the carbon emission of the production of  batteries using recycled materials from direct physical recycling is  51.8% lower than that using raw materials. However, the physical  recycling method is not mature in technology and is still in the stage of  small-scale experiments. Developing efficient and mature physical  recycling methods for large-scale applications is critical to reducing  carbon emissions.  3.4. Conclusions  In this study, the cradle-to-cradle LCA of the LIBs for EVs in China is  conducted, and some valuable carbon reduction measures are given.  Moreover, the carbon emission of LIBs production under different  electricity mixes is examined in the next 30 years for China ' s carbon  neutral strategy. The main conclusions of this study can be summarized  as follows:   (1) Under the current electricity mix dominated by thermal power in  China, the carbon emission of the NCM811 battery in the pro­ duction stage is 91.21 kg CO 2 -eq/kWh, in which the production  of cathode contributes the most, followed by battery assembly.  The carbon emission during battery usage is 154.1 kg CO 2 -eq/  kWh, mainly from the indirect carbon emission generated by the  electricity consumed by the battery. The carbon emission of  battery recycling by hydrometallurgy is 2.68 kg CO 2 -eq/kWh,  which is an ideal recycling method at present.   (2) Increasing the green proportion in the electricity mix and  improving production efficiency can reduce carbon emissions.  Under China ' s electricity mix in 2050, the carbon emissions in the  production and use stages of batteries will be reduced by 74.8%  and 75.1%, respectively. When the battery production efficiency  is increased from 30 ppm to 50 ppm, the carbon emission of  battery production can be reduced by about 40%. The remanu­ factured batteries using recycled materials can reduce carbon by  51.8%. In short, green electricity, improving production effi­ ciency, and strengthening battery recycling are necessary carbon  reduction measures.   (3) The carbon emission reduction rate of battery production in  China will accelerate after the carbon peak in 2030. By 2050, the  carbon emission of battery production will be 84.9% lower than  that of battery production in 2020. It is predicted that carbon  neutralization in LIB production can be realized by optimizing  the electricity mix and developing CCUs, DAC, V2G, and other  technologies before 2060.  In future work, battery ecological indicators and battery technology  factors will be coupled to investigate the sustainability of the battery.  For example, the nonlinear ageing of the battery will be considered in  the calculation of carbon emissions during battery use.
