Moreover, coke oven gas purification requires additional energy and auxiliary  materials, resulting in significant indirect emissions. The purpose of this study to assess the environmental impact of coke pro- duction based on the data of a real coking plant and compare four different operation scenarios using model optimised data. Method   The “cradle to gate” life cycle assessment of the investigated coking plant were conducted by subdivision method.  The functional unit was determined as the production of 1 ton of coke. Four scenarios were compared in this research work.  The first one assumed normal operating conditions (scenario 1), the second one assumed environmentally optimised condi- tions as defined by the steady-state model of the gas purification technology (scenario 2), the third one assumed energetically  optimised conditions as also defined by the model (scenario 3), and the fourth one assumed no gas purification technology  at all (scenario 4). The study was performed using Sphera's Life cycle assessment software (formerly known as GaBi) based  on ISO 14040 and ISO 14044 standards, and nine environmental impact categories were considered. Results and discussion   Besides coke production, transportation and gas purification had the most highest relative impact  in most categories, with an average of 32%, 20%, and 12%, respectively. Among the input auxiliary materials, electricity  contributed the most to the investigated categories (avg. 62%), followed by steam (avg. 26%) and process water (avg. 11%).  The comparative analysis revealed that, compared to the base scenario, the energetically optimised scenario had the low- est environmental impact in the most considered categories (e.g. GWP decreased from 546.54 to 544.15 kg ­CO 2  eq). The  optimisation reduces the energy demand of the plant, while the pollutant emissions do not exceed the standards; therefore,  the direct emissions do not lead to a significant increase in environmental impact. The sensitivity analysis shows that a 10%  reduction in electricity consumption significantly decreases the environmental impact of all the sub-processes. Conclusions   This is the first study to perform a coke production life cycle assessment using optimised data calculated by a  process simulator alongside real plant data. Through this, the intention was to provide valuable insights into the environmental  significance of coke oven gas cleaning technology. Keywords   Life cycle assessment · Coke production · Coke oven gas · Gas purification · Real plant data · Optimised data 1  Introduction Coke is one of the most essential raw materials for pig iron  production (Babich and Senk  2019 ). Coke is produced by  heating the coking coal mixture at high temperatures in air- tight chambers, while coke oven gas (COG) is formed as a  by-product. The gas's quantity and composition depend on  the volatile content of the coal blend and the temperature  used in the coke oven chambers (Loison et al.  1989 ; Manka  1979 ). Raw coke oven gas contains high-value components  and contaminants (Bermúdez et al.  2013 ). The concentration of polluting com- ponents in the gas should be removed as much as possible or  at least reduced to below environmental standards since the  main impurities ­(H 2 S and ­NH 3 ) cause corrosion of equip- ment and pipelines (Caillat  2017 ) and produce ­SO x  and ­NO x   during combustion (Yen et al.  2015 ). The purified coke oven  gas contains the highest percentage of hydrogen and meth- ane (Zhang et al.  2020 ), so it has almost as high heating  value as natural gas (Razzaq et al.  2013 ). Coke oven gas  is, therefore, an excellent substitute for natural gas and can  provide a significant part of the energy needs of integrated  iron and steel plants (Sui et al.  2023 ). In addition, various  on-site and off-site application methods have been examined,  including combined heat and power (CHP) plants, methanol  synthesis, hydrogen, ammonia and syngas production (Raz- zaq et al.  2013 ). However, coke production is a highly energy-intensive  process (Porzio et al.  2014 ) and is also environmentally pol- luting as several pollutants are emitted into the air, water  and soil (Pilarczyk et al.  2013 ). In addition, the purification  process of the by-product requires additional energy and  auxiliary materials, leading to significant indirect emissions.  A comprehensive life cycle assessment can help understand  and identify the environmental hotspots and the potential  for technological improvement in coke production and oven  gas purification. Life cycle assessment studies the environmental factors  and potential impacts associated with products, from raw  material sourcing through production, consumption and final  disposal. It is a method that partly inventories the inputs  and outputs of the system of processes related to a product,  evaluates the potential environmental impacts connected to  them, and interprets the results of the inventory analysis and  the impact assessment phases from the perspective of the  purpose of the study (Curran  2016 ). Due to its benefits, LCA  method is widely used in different industry evaluations, for  example in the construction (Essaghouri et al.  2023 ; Zhang  et al.  2019 ), food (Takacs and Borrion  2020 ; Cai et al.  2022 ),  chemical (Aldaco et al.  2019 ; Khoo et al.  2018 ) or iron and  steel industries (Leão et al.  2023 ; Olmez et al.  2016 ; Moz- iraji et al.  2023 ). In recent years, more and more studies have been pub- lished concerning the environmental impact and life cycle  assessment of coking plants and coke oven gas utilisation. Li et al. ( 2020 ) created a holistic cradle-to-gate LCA on  coking production using the subdivision method. The results  showed that COG purification contributed to air emissions  of the entire coke production to a similar or even more sig- nificant than the coke production process. Concerning COG  purification, the dominating contributors were ammonia  recovery and blowing cooler system units due to the signifi- cant steam and electricity consumption in these units. Liu  and Yuan ( 2016 ) presented a cradle-to-gate LCA to deter- mine a Chinese-specific LCI of coke production and to com- pare coke wet (CWQ) and dry (CDQ) quenching methods.  The results show a reduction of up to 15% in the selected  environmental impact categories for coke dry quenching  technology. Li et al. ( 2018 ) performed a cradle-to-gate LCA  of COG-based methanol production and compared it with  two other production routes. The results revealed that the  coal route has the highest environmental impact among the  selected categories, followed by the COG route, while the  natural gas route has the lowest. It was also concluded that  the COG-based route has the lowest production cost. Chen  et al. ( 2019 ) performed a similar LCA study on the three  types of methanol production routes. García et al. ( 2019 )  applied the LCA of the cogeneration process with waste  gases (COG and Linz-Donawitz converter gas) of an inte- grated iron and steel plant. Five different scenarios were  examined, varying the amount of waste and natural gas  burned in the cogeneration process. It was found that it is  important to minimise the consumption of natural gas and  that waste gases are worse than natural gas in terms of ­CO 2   emissions, but it is still better to use them in the cogenera- tion process than to burn them in the torch. Li et al. ( 2021 )  conducted a comparative LCA of coke oven gas-based liqui- fied natural gas processes and showed that the decarbonisa- tion process has worse environmental performance than the  methanation process since less product is produced. Besides,  it has been proven that ­H 2  extraction in the methanation pro- cess is more economically desirable than without ­H 2  extrac- tion. Ren et al. ( 2022 ) quantified the environmental and  economic impacts of three different COG utilisation routes:  COG-to-methanol, COG-to-hydrogen and COG-to-synthetic  natural gas. It was presented that the COG-to-hydrogen route  has the least environmental and economic impact of all three  utilisation routes. Li and Cheng ( 2020 ) created LCA to compare traditional  and COG-based hydrogen production routes' carbon emis- sions, energy consumption and economic costs. COG-based  hydrogen production has higher economic competitiveness.  Li et al. ( 2023 ) conducted a comparative LCA to evaluate  the environmental impacts of three different COG routes  for ammonia production, including a single process and two  coproduction processes (ammonia with methanol or LNG).  Based on the results, the coproduction of ammonia with the  methanol route has the best environmental performance of  the three routes. Liu and Guo ( 2023 ) compared four COG  utilisation schemes through the LCA method: COG-to- hydrogen, COG-to-natural gas, COG-to-methanol and COG- to-power. The results revealed that COG-to-power performs  better in all aspects and is the most competitive utilisation  scheme, while COG-to-methanol is the least competitive. Di  et al. ( 2024 ) also compared different COG utilisation routes  and concluded that the COG-to-synthetic ammonia route is  The International Journal of Life Cycle Assessment	 the preferred option for balancing environmental and eco- nomic benefits. Basically, a method was presented that can be used  for operational decision making in real technologies, taking  into account different aspects. 2  Process description The investigated process is shown schematically in Fig.  1 . Coking coals are transported into the plant by bulk car- riers or by rail, then waggons are emptied with the use of  waggon tipplers, and coals are stored in silos before use. The  different kinds of coals are blended, crushed, homogenised  and charged into the chambers of the coke oven batteries.  The coal blend spends 16-24 h in the airtight hot chambers.  After the degasification, the coke is pushed out of the cham- bers and cooled by inert gas in the dry quenching technology  or by water in the wet quenching technology. The absorbed  heat by the inert gas in dry quenching technology is used  to produce high-temperature, superheated steam in heat  recovery heat exchangers, which is converted into electric- ity and low-pressure steam by a steam turbine. The produced  electricity covers 30-40% of the plant's electricity demand,  while the produced steam provides 70-80% of the steam  needs. The cooled coke is graded with different coke screens  and transferred to the blast furnace. During the degasification, 45,000-60,000 ­m 3 /h of raw  coke oven gas is produced, which is cooled down by water  spray directly and sucked out of the chamber via separa- tors, electric tar separators and pre-coolers by gas-exhausters  and transferred for further gas cleaning. In this process, coal  tar and water are separated from the gas. Tar is a valuable  by-product, while the water is used in the gas purification  scrubbers, and surplus water is treated in the wastewater  treatment plant by chemical and biological processes. The  raw gas is transferred into the gas purification plant through  a ­H 2 S and two ­NH 3  scrubbers. The washing liquid leaving  the scrubbers is regenerated, while the vapour fraction of the  regenaration is fed to the closed Claus plant, where the heat  of the crack reactor produces steam, which covers 2-5% of  the steam demand. The cleaned coke oven  gas from the light oil washing towers is partly used for heat- ing the batteries, and the remainder is used for energetic  purposes. 2.1  Gas purification process and model Gas purification is the most important process for fur- ther use of coke oven gas. For this pupose, various tech- nologies are used, mainly absorption systems. The most  commonly applied purification technology is aqueous  ammonia absorption, but several technologies using more  different absorbents have also emerged in this industry,  such as Stretford, Takahx or Sulfiban processes (Kohl and  Nielsen  1997 ). Further use of inadequately cleaned gas  can lead to serious problems, such as exceeding environ- mental emission limits or increasing equipment corrosion.  Therefore, this study focuses primarily on this process,  and based on our previous work, different scenarios are  examined. The gas purification process can be separated into two  sections: gas cleaning and regeneration. The gas cleaning  section contains an ­H 2 S and two ­NH 3  scrubbers, while the  regeneration section has two stripping and two deacidi- fication columns. In the scrubbers, three kinds of wash- ing liquids are used: stripped water (the bottom product  of the stripping column), de-acidified water (the bottom  product of deacidification column and has high ­NH 3  con- tent) and coal water (formed from the moisture content of  the coal blend and separated from COG with the help of  separators). When the scrubbers are not in operation due  to maintenance or malfunction, the stripping columns must  still be applied since the pre-treatment of the coal water is  done here, and only then can it be fed to the wastewater  treatment plant. Radó-Fóty et al. ( 2022 ) developed the steady-state pro- cess model of the gas cleaning section and performed two  optimisation tasks: environmental and energetic optimisa- tion. The steady-state process model was built in Aspen  Plus, and it was connected to MATLAB to accomplish  the optimisation tasks. The detailed description of the  model and the objective functions used are given in the  referenced publication. Environmental optimisation means  minimising the content of ­H 2 S and ­NH 3  in the purified  COG. This goal can be reached by decreasing the water  stream temperature and increasing the washing liquids'  flow rate. Energetic optimisation aims to reduce energy  consumption and, at the same time, ensure compliance  with emission standards (European Commission  2012 ).  For this purpose, the relatively high temperature of the  water streams should be used, which is why less auxiliary  energy is required. The results of the two optimization tasks were used in this  study for scenario 2 and scenario 3. The calculated results,  namely the amount of washing liquids and the composition  of the purified coke oven gas, were used as input parameters  for the life cycle assessment in Sphera's Life cycle assess- ment software. 3  Materials and methods This study was conducted by ISO 14040 (International  Standard Organization  2006a ) and ISO 14044 (International  Standard Organization  2006b ) standards, so it includes four  interrelated phases: •	  Definition of goal and scope •	  Life cycle inventory analysis (LCI) •	  Life cycle impact assessment (LCIA) •	  Life cycle interpretation (this phase is discussed in the 4.  Results and discussion section) 3.1  Goal and scope Defining the goal and scope is the first phase at the begin- ning of the study, before data collection, and it is an essen- tial part of LCA methodology since it is where the accurate  approach to be followed is determined (Curran  2017 ). This study aimed to assess the environmental impact of  coke production and compare four different scenarios. The  four scenarios are as follows: normal operating conditions  (scenario 1), environmentally optimised conditions of the  gas purification process (scenario 2), energetically optimised  conditions of the gas purification process (scenario 3) and  not operating gas purification process conditions (scenario  4). Based on Fig.  2 , the boundaries of the system were  defined. Thus, the previously introduced processes were  considered in eight sub-processes: transportation, loading  and storage, coking process, coke quenching and screening,  cooling and tar recovery, gas purification, wastewater treat- ment and light oil recovery. Malfunctions and accidents were  not taken into account in the assessment. For each sub-processes, the energy and auxiliary flows  were modelled using country-specific (e.g. electricity grid  mix, process steam from natural gas) or, if not available in  the database, European data (e.g. compressed air). Transportation:  Only coal transportation was considered  in this sub-process due to the long distance. While the used  auxiliary and other raw materials are purchased relatively  close to the plant, and the products are used locally or sold  nearby, transporting these materials is negligible compared  	 The International Journal of Life Cycle Assessment to coal. Forty percent of the used coals are transported from  other continents with an average distance of 11,500 km by  bulk carriers, modelled by “GLO: Bulk commodity carrier.  average. ocean-going”. The rest of the coal used is delivered  to the plant from European countries by rail, from an aver- age distance of 726 km. This form of transportation was  modelled by “GLO: Rail transport cargo—Electric. average  train. gross tonne weight 1.000 t/726 t payload capacity”.  Electricity and heavy fuel oil are consumed during these two  kinds of transportation. Loading and storage:  The coal-filled waggons arriving  at the plant are tippled, loaded into silos, blended, crushed  and homogenised. These processes operate in a closed sys- tem and use dust extraction equipment for those stages that  emit dust (e.g. crushing) so that dust emissions into the air  are minimised. The dust removed in the separators is added  to the coal blend. In addition, sludge from the wastewater  treatment plant is mixed with the coal blend. The residues  from the tar separation (coal tar residue and coal tar pitch  residue) are also blended into the coal blend. Coking process:  This sub-process consists of two coke  oven batteries and the coking machines responsible for coal  loading (charging car) and coke pushing (pusher machine  and coke guide car). Coke pushing emission control system  limits the release of dust during chambers are pushing. The  cleaned coke oven gas is used in excess air to heat the coke  oven batteries, which causes emissions (e.g. ­NO x , ­SO 2 , ­CO 2 ,  particulate). Coke quenching and screening:  Two types of coke  quenching, dry and wet quenching and coke screening, were  considered in this stage. Dry quenching, as described previ- ously, produces electricity and steam, reducing the technol- ogy's energy purchase demand. The coke dust generated  during coke classification is separated and mixed back into  the coal blend. As the quantity of coke dust is minor com- pared to the coal used, it is not specially accounted for in  the analysis. Cooling and tar recovery:  The raw coke oven gas is  cooled and separated in a closed system, so under normal  operating conditions, no pollutants can be released into the  air, water or soil. The residues of this process are mixed with  the coal blend, but this is negligible compared to the total  amount of coal used and is therefore not taken into account  in this study. Gas purification:  This sub-process is the most impor- tant for this study. Like cooling and tar recovery, it is a  closed system, too, so no direct emissions can occur. In  this stage, the largest amount of auxiliary material, sodium  hydroxide (2.5 kg/ton of coke), is used to regenerate the  washing liquids. Due to the closed Claus technology, in  Fig. 2    System boundary of the process The International Journal of Life Cycle Assessment	 the cracking reactor, the parameters are adjusted so that  the residual reaction gases at the end of the process can  be recycled into the coke oven gas system. This means  residual gases do not need to be burned in a flare, and no  flue gas is emitted. Wastewater treatment:  The surplus process water is  treated at the wastewater treatment plant before being dis- charged to the receiving water. The discharged water is regu- larly monitored to ensure that the emissions (e.g. nitrate,  BTEX, ­BOD 5 , PAH, sulphide, cyanide) are below environ- mental standards. Various auxiliary materials are used for  wastewater treatment (e.g. flocculant, defoamer, hydrated  lime, glucose), the amount of which is below 1 kg/ton of  coke, but to describe the process more precisely, it has been  taken into account. Light oil recovery:  This is the final stage of the gas treat- ment process. The quality of the washing oil used for light  oil recovery is modified by the polymerisation reactions that  occur during heating. These changes are technologically  highly negative because, on the one hand, the substances  precipitated by polymerisation can cause blockages in the  pipes, and, on the other hand, the ability of the oil to absorb  benzene is reduced. In order to avoid this, the washing oil  must be regenerated. The residual polymerised oil is fed into  the tar recovery process. Further- more, mass allocation can be reliably applied to all prod- ucts, unlike energy allocation, as sulphur is not an energy  substance, while economic allocation cannot be used due  to lack of information. Figure  3   shows the relative contribution of each sub-process to the  nine previously introduced environmental impact categories  to explore the individual contribution of each sub-process. For five of the nine selected categories, the coking process  exhibits a significantly higher impact than the other sub-pro- cesses. The coking process has the most significant impact on  GWP (85%), followed by AP (80%), POCP (74%), EP (63%)  and finally HTP inf. (28%). In the other categories, the impact  of the coking process is below 10%. The reason is mainly  attributed to the pollutant emission caused by the combus- tion of coke oven gas (Wang et al.  2018 ; Cheng et al.  2024 ). Transportation has a remarkable contribution to all nine  categories, but the largest percentage can be observed  for FAETP (41%). This can be explained by the fact that  almost half of the coking coal used comes from other con- tinents via bulk carriers. In addition, several coke producers, particularly small- and  medium-sized companies, tend to have low environmental  awareness and show a lack of environmental responsibility.  Therefore, it is important that environmental authorities and  policy decision-makers establish appropriate restrictions on  plants that do not make their operation impossible but set  reasonable limits on the operating conditions. In the present economic situation and environmental  regulations, multi-objective optimisation should be pre- ferred when designing new plants and developing and oper- ating existing ones. LCA based on process simulation results  is particularly effective in supporting the sustainable opera- tion of existing plants in the context of dynamically chang- ing energy prices and tightening environmental regulations.  Identifying the environmental impact of critical process stages  and state variables during technology development allows to  integrate the response to unexpected external effects into deci- sion processes. By analysing the technology in detail, differ- ent impacts can be modelled in advance, and environmental  impacts can be determined at each stage of the process. Based on the results of the base case scenario 1, the cok- ing process had the largest impact in several categories (AP,  EP, GWP, HTP inf. and POCP). In addition, transportation,  gas purification and cooling and tar recovery sub-processes  had significant environmental impacts. It can be concluded  that electricity had the largest contribution to the environ- mental impact categories among the input auxiliary materi- als, followed by steam and process water. The results of the basic assessment were compared with  the results of three different scenarios (scenario 2, environ- mentally optimised gas purification; scenario 3, energetically  optimised gas purification; scenario 4, no gas purification).  In the coking process, the relative impact decreased for every  category in scenario 2 (between 0.6 and 6.7%), while it  increased in the other two scenarios (2.2-13.5% for scenario  3 and 3.1-64.2% for scenario 4) because the composition  of the coke oven gas used to heat the chambers changed. In  contrast, the opposite trend can be observed for gas puri- fication, as scenario 2 requires more energy investment to  clean the gas properly, increasing the process's environmental  impact. The growth for scenario 2 varied between 9.0 and  33.2%. However, as the gas purification rate decreased or  was eliminated, less energy was needed for the plant part.  Thus, a reduction of between 8.9 and 29.7% was observed  for scenario 3 and between 45.7 and 91.3% for scenario 4. Comparing the environmental impacts of the whole  technology for the different scenarios, it can be observed  that, compared to the base case, there is a reduction in 3  categories for scenario 2, 5 categories for scenario 3 and 4  categories for scenario 4. The largest increase is obtained  in scenario 4 for AP (130%), EP (56%) and POCP (113%).  The comparison of the results indicated that the energetically  optimised condition is the most favourable scenario in terms  of environmental impacts also. The reason for this is that the  environmental impact categories increase by 10% or less,  while in the other categories, the decrease ranges from 2 to  7% leading to an overall better performance.
